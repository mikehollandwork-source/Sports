# "A number he has never missed" — does it hold?

_Read-only. A prior MINIMUM is not a floor: it is the smallest value in a sample, so it drifts down as the sample grows and the chance of clearing it is below 100% by construction. Every row below is out of sample and in date order — the minimum is taken from starts BEFORE the night being scored, and a start needs 8 earlier ones to count._

- starters with 8+ prior starts: **142**
- scored starts: **891**

| line | clears | n | what a 5-leg ticket loses to this leg |
|---|---|---|---|
| his exact prior minimum | **92.0%** | 790 | 8.0% of the ticket |
| one under it (safer) | **97.0%** | 569 | 3.0% of the ticket |
| two under it (safest) | **98.1%** | 310 | 1.9% of the ticket |

## How much the sample matters

_If the minimum were a floor, more prior starts would make it safer. It does the opposite: the more starts a pitcher has, the lower his minimum has been dragged, and the rate does not improve the way certainty would._

| prior starts | clears his prior minimum | n |
|---|---|---|
| 8–11 | **93.3%** | 507 |
| 12–15 | **92.9%** | 337 |
| 16–19 | **89.4%** | 47 |

## What this does to the proposed ticket

_The leg is **92.0%** (± 1.9%), not 100%. On a five-leg ticket every leg multiplies, so this one alone removes **8.0%** of the ticket's chance._

_The useful part: a leg this short is priced near certainty too, so it adds very little to the payout while taking that bite out of the probability. It is the leg most likely to be worth replacing with nothing — a four-leg ticket at the same other four legs pays slightly less and wins more often._
